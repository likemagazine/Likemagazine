INDUSTRY & INFRASTRUCTURE · THE WORLDWIDE GUIDE

THE WORLD’S ELECTRIC COMPANIES

By Michael de la Force · Editor-in-Chief, LIKE® Magazine · Industry & Infrastructure · October 2026

Electricity is the one product every reader of this magazine uses every hour, made and delivered by an industry organised in strikingly different ways. In Florida and Virginia it is still the classic regulated utility, earning a return set by a state commission; in Texas and across PJM it is a merchant business that lives or dies by the wholesale price; in Bilbao, Rome and Paris it is the integrated European major, owning wires and wind farms on several continents; and in Beijing, Seoul, New Delhi, Riyadh and Johannesburg it is the state itself, running the grid on which a whole economy turns. This guide maps thirty of the companies that keep the lights on — who leads each of them in October 2026, under exact published titles; what they generate, carry and sell; how many customers they serve and on what basis that number is counted; what they earned in their last reported year; and how a customer, a regulator or a competitor actually reaches them. Every figure is dated and sourced in its entry, capacity bases are stated rather than merged, and every field that could not be verified to that standard is named, in the entry and again at the end, under Named Gaps.

The thirty are grouped by posture, not by size: eleven integrated utilities that generate, carry and sell; seven regulated utilities whose returns are set by commissions; three generation and merchant houses with no wires at all; three grid and transmission companies that own no power stations worth the name; and six state utilities, from EDF — renationalised in 2023 and building Europe's next nuclear fleet — to Eskom, whose turnaround South Africa watches by the week. State Grid Corporation of China appears as a context entry, honestly framed: by revenue it is the largest utility organisation on earth, and its English-language disclosure is thinner than that of the listed companies around it, which is itself reported rather than papered over.

Established in 1951 in the post-war restructuring of Japan's electricity industry as the integrated utility for the Kantō region; operator of the Fukushima Daiichi plant, whose 2011 accident reshaped the company and Japanese energy policy; the state-backed Nuclear Damage Compensation and Decommissioning Facilitation Corporation became its majority voting shareholder in 2012, making TEPCO a state-controlled utility in substance — the basis of its category here. Restructured in 2016 into a holding company with fuel and power generation (with JERA for thermal), power grid, and energy retail companies. The utility carrying the heaviest single responsibility in this guide: decommissioning Fukushima Daiichi and compensating its victims, while still supplying the largest metropolitan economy in the world. Kobayakawa has led TEPCO since 2017, its youngest president at appointment. The Kashiwazaki-Kariwa restart process and Fukushima decommissioning costs dominate every TEPCO financial statement and are flagged in its results.
