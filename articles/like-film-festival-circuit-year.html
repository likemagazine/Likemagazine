FILM FESTIVALS · THE CIRCUIT

THE CIRCUIT YEAR: HOW THE WORLD'S FESTIVALS SEQUENCE THE FILM BUSINESS

By Michael de la Force · Editor-in-Chief, LIKE® Magazine · Film Festivals · October 2026

The film business keeps its calendar in festivals. A film's commercial life — whether it finds a distributor at all, what it is worth, which prizes it can plausibly chase — is decided less in boardrooms than in a sequence of cities that begins each January in the Utah snow and ends each November in Amsterdam, Thessaloniki and Cairo. Thirty-eight festivals anchor LIKE® Magazine's worldwide guide. Together they form a circuit, and the circuit has a logic: each stop decides something different.

JANUARY: THE YEAR OPENS IN THE SNOW

The year begins at the Sundance Film Festival, founded in 1978 as the Utah/US Film Festival by Robert Redford and his partners, and run today by festival director Eugene Hernandez. Sundance is the discovery engine of American independent cinema: for its final Utah edition, held Jan. 22 to Feb. 1, 2026, the festival weighed 16,201 submissions and selected 90 feature films. What Sundance decides is distribution itself. It attaches no sales market, yet the year's first great acquisitions are negotiated in its lodges and screening rooms; at the 2026 edition, A24 paid a reported price of more than $10 million for U.S. rights to Olivia Wilde's The Invite, the festival's biggest on-site deal. In January 2027 the festival begins again in a new home — Boulder, Colorado, Jan. 21–31 — the most consequential change of address in the circuit's modern history.

The same weeks belong to Europe's filmmakers' festivals. The International Film Festival Rotterdam, directed by Vanja Kaludjercic, pairs its Tiger Competition for new directors with CineMart, the co-production market it founded in 1984: Rotterdam decides which of the coming years' art films get financed. In Sweden, the Göteborg Film Festival — artistic director Pia Lundberg — drew 287,000 visits in 2026 and runs the Nordic Film Market and the television gathering TV Drama Vision, the Nordic industry's winter meeting.

FEBRUARY: BERLIN SETS THE EUROPEAN TERMS

The Berlin International Film Festival, directed by Tricia Tuttle, is the year's first top-tier competition and its first full-scale marketplace. The 76th Berlinale, Feb. 12–22, 2026, showed 278 films from 80 countries and awarded the Golden Bear to Yellow Letters, directed by İlker Çatak. Alongside it, the European Film Market — directed by Tanja Meissner within the festival's Berlinale Pro* umbrella — opens the international sales year, and the Berlinale Co-Production Market matches projects to financiers. What Berlin decides is the shape of the European arthouse year: which films European distributors will carry, and which unfinished films will be finished at all. In 2026 Berlin also reached into genre, launching an EFM Frontières Focus with Montreal's genre market — a sign of where the business is widening.

MARCH TO MAY: THE SPECIALISTS, THEN THE SUMMIT

Spring belongs first to the specialists and the regional markets. CPH:DOX in Copenhagen, led by artistic director Niklas Engstrøm, opens documentary's year each March with its financing forum; the Hong Kong International Film Festival marked its 50th edition in April 2026 alongside the Hong Kong – Asia Film Financing Forum, the spring financing point for Asian cinema; and Toronto's Hot Docs, North America's largest documentary festival, returned its Hot Docs Forum in 2026. In Austin, SXSW runs its film festival inside a combined conference of music, comedy and technology — a premiere at SXSW is a cultural event first and a market event second.

Then, in May, the circuit reaches its summit. The Cannes Film Festival — president Iris Knobloch, general delegate Thierry Frémaux — received 2,541 submitted feature films from 141 countries for its 79th edition and awarded the Palme d'Or to Fjord, directed by Cristian Mungiu. Cannes decides hierarchy. A place in its Competition reprices a film in every territory on earth, and its Marché du Film, the world's largest film market, is where the year's international distribution is substantially written. Every other festival in the calendar is, in some measure, programming toward Cannes or recovering from it.

SUMMER: DISCOVERY, REGIONS AND THE GENRES

The summer festivals sort the year's remaining business by region and by kind. June brings New York's Tribeca Festival — co-founder and chief executive Jane Rosenthal, festival director Cara Cusumano — and the Shanghai International Film Festival, China's only FIAPF-accredited competitive festival, which drew a record 4,100 submissions in 2026: Shanghai is the circuit's gateway to the Chinese market. In Annecy, the animation world holds its entire business in a single June week, festival and Mifa market together. July belongs to Karlovy Vary, whose Eastern Promises industry days serve the cinemas of Central and Eastern Europe, and to Montreal's Fantasia, artistic director Mitch Davis, where genre cinema's discovery economics are at their most visible: a Fantasia world premiere of The Undertone in 2025 set off a bidding war that A24 won within days.

August is the auteur month. The Locarno Film Festival — artistic director Giona A. Nazzaro, president Maja Hoffmann — showed 233 films in 2026 and runs its Open Doors platform, whose current four-year cycle is dedicated to 42 countries of the African continent: Locarno decides futures for cinemas far from the market's centre. The Sarajevo Film Festival, founded during the siege of its city in 1995 and directed by Jovan Marjanović, is Southeast Europe's meeting point through its CineLink Industry Days. In Edinburgh, the world's oldest continually running festival gives its two centrepiece prizes — the £50,000 Sean Connery Prize among them — by audience vote, an honour with cash attached. And in Melbourne, the 37°South Market finances Australian and New Zealand production while the festival's Bright Horizons Award, at A$140,000, stands among the richest film prizes in the world.

SEPTEMBER AND OCTOBER: THE AUTUMN LAUNCH

The circuit's decisive season opens on the Venice Lido. The Venice International Film Festival — artistic director Alberto Barbera, whose mandate the Biennale extended through 2028 — awarded its 83rd Golden Lion to Woman Unknown, directed by May el-Toukhy. On the same September weekend, in the Colorado mountains, the Telluride Film Festival, executive director Julie Huntsinger, operates on opposite principles: no prizes, no press conferences, a lineup announced the day before it opens. Telluride's currency is the judgement of the several thousand people who make the trip — a constituency that overlaps conspicuously with the people who vote for the season's awards.

The Toronto International Film Festival — chief executive Cameron Bailey — converts that judgement into data. Its People's Choice Award, voted by the largest festival audience in North America and won in 2026 by La bola negra, is the awards season's first hard signal, tracked by every campaign strategist in Los Angeles. In 2026 Toronto also formalised its commercial role, opening TIFF: The Market at the Metro Toronto Convention Centre. The launch then crosses back to Europe for the San Sebastián International Film Festival and its Golden Shell — the bridge between European and Latin American cinema, where director José Luis Rebordinos presided over his final edition in 2026 before Maialen Beloki succeeds him in 2027 — and closes in New York, where the New York Film Festival, artistic director Dennis Lim, remains proudly non-competitive: a curated Main Slate that certifies the season's canon for critics and audiences.

October and November are the working months. London's BFI London Film Festival is the public's October anchor; Hamburg pairs its audience festival with new industry days; in South Korea the Busan International Film Festival and its Asian Contents & Film Market form Asia's autumn marketplace — 3,158 registered market participants in 2026 — and Busan's top prize became, under FIAPF's 2026 accreditation reform, an Academy Award–qualifying route for best international feature film, the only such route in Asia. Tokyo follows with the TIFFCOM market; the Viennale offers a deliberate counter-model, a fortnight of films with no market at all; Los Angeles gathers the season at AFI Fest; and in November the IDFA festival in Amsterdam — artistic director Isabel Arrate Fernandez — holds documentary's financing summit: 859 projects entered its Forum in 2026, and 47 were chosen. Thessaloniki's AGORA, Tallinn's industry days, Cairo's industry programme and Mar del Plata's historic competition complete the year.

WHAT EACH STOP DECIDES

Read as one calendar, the circuit divides its labour cleanly. Discovery belongs to Sundance, Rotterdam, Edinburgh and Fantasia. Finance belongs to the markets that ride on festivals' backs — CineMart, the Berlinale Co-Production Market, the HAF in Hong Kong, Busan's project market, Melbourne's 37°South, Locarno's Open Doors and the IDFA Forum. Distribution belongs to the Marché du Film, the European Film Market and, from 2026, TIFF: The Market. Consecration belongs to Cannes, Venice and New York. And awards positioning — the season's final economy — belongs to the six weeks from Venice to New York each autumn.

Prediction: the evidence of 2026 points one way. Toronto built a market; Berlin's market built a genre showcase; Busan's prize acquired Oscar-qualifying power. The festivals that thrive in the decade ahead will be those that own a piece of the business transacted in their name — and the open question of the next circuit year is the one Sundance will answer in Boulder: whether a festival's gravity lives in the event itself, or in the place that made it.
