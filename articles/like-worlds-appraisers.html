AUCTIONS & COLLECTING · THE WORLDWIDE GUIDE

THE WORLD'S APPRAISERS & VALUATION FIRMS

By Michael de la Force · Editor-in-Chief, LIKE® Magazine · Auctions & Collecting · October 2026

Before anything in an auction room can be sold, someone has to say what it is worth — and sign the saying. That signature is the least visible and most load-bearing object in the entire art and assets economy. It sits under the catalogue estimate, the insurance schedule, the estate filing, the bank loan and the board's fairness opinion. This guide maps the signatories: the firms that appraise, the divisions that value, and the professional bodies whose initials after a name are the reason the number is believed.

The list covers 28 entries in four sections: art and collectibles; real estate; automotive, equipment and industrial assets; and the generalist, cross-asset practices. Four entries are not firms at all, and say so in their names and in every field — the Appraisers Association of America, the International Society of Appraisers, the American Society of Appraisers and RICS are professional bodies: they credential, educate and discipline appraisers, and appraise nothing themselves. Fifteen entries are divisions or practices of a parent, and are framed as divisions, never as independent firms. One entry, Hagerty, is a listed insurance and membership company included for its valuation tools — the collector-car market's reference prices — and framed honestly as that, not as an appraisal firm. Where a practice publishes no head, no volume and no address, the entry reads "not published." A gap printed plainly is worth more than a name invented confidently: that is, in miniature, the entire ethic of the profession this guide covers.

THE YEARS THE APPRAISERS CHANGED HANDS

Valuation is supposed to be the still centre of the market. Lately it has been one of its busiest trading floors. In 2025 Winston Art Group, the largest independent American art appraisal firm, merged with the data company Artory to form Winston Artory Group — the appraiser's eye, checked against fifty million auction records — and within months its president was describing a $15 billion year of valuations in a falling market, including a $1 million print revalued to $300,000 at an insurer's request. Pall Mall Art Advisors, which appraised some $12 billion a year, no longer exists as a standalone firm: London's Fine Art Group acquired it in 2021 and trades under its own name, a fact this guide states in the entry's title rather than discovering in a footnote. The lender-appointed platforms consolidated too: ORIX USA took a majority of Hilco Global in 2025; Oaktree took a majority of the old Great American appraisal business, now GA Group, which appointed co-presidents of its Advisory & Valuation business on 20 October 2026, two days before this guide closed; Coalesce Capital took a majority of Marshall & Stevens in December 2025 and a new chief executive followed in 2026; and Newmark simply bought its way across Europe, acquiring valuation firms in Paris and Germany in eight months. Even the divisions changed drivers: JLL appointed a new chief executive of Value & Risk Advisory in October 2025 with a mandate to rebuild valuation around AI, and CBRE placed its global valuations business under its Advisory chief operating officer from January 2026.

Underneath the trading sits the older architecture, and it is worth naming because every credentials column in this guide refers back to it. In the United States, appraisals run to USPAP, the Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice, and the designations of three bodies founded across a century — the Appraisers Association (1949) for art, the International Society of Appraisers (1979) for personal property, and the American Society of Appraisers (1936) for everything, machinery and jewellery included. Internationally the reference texts are the IVS of the International Valuation Standards Council and the RICS Red Book, the global standards of the Royal Institution of Chartered Surveyors, founded in 1868 and still writing the rules from Parliament Square. A valuation is trusted, in the end, because a named person with a revocable credential signed it under one of those regimes. The firms below compete; the regimes are what they compete inside.

HOW TO READ THE GUIDE

Three cautions, printed once and applied everywhere. First, form matters: a division of a listed brokerage, an independent partnership of appraisers and a professional body are three different animals, and each entry's category says which animal it is. Second, scale figures are published, dated and scoped — Pall Mall's $12 billion a year (2021, its own practice) and its parent's $20 billion (today, the whole group) both appear, precisely so they are not confused, and where no figure is published the entry says so. Third, the entry route is the reader's real question, so every entry answers it: whom an insurer, an executor, a lender or a collector actually calls, and how the engagement begins.
