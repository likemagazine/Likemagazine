TECHNOLOGY · THE WORLDWIDE GUIDE

THE WORLD'S SYSTEMS INTEGRATORS

By Michael de la Force · Editor-in-Chief, LIKE® Magazine · Technology · October 2026

Every large company and government runs on systems somebody else built. The cloud migration, the new ERP, the AI programme, the service desk that never sleeps — behind almost all of it stands a systems integrator. This guide, the first for LIKE® Magazine's new Technology department, maps that world firm by firm: 31 integrators, from Accenture's 814,000 people to the focused public-sector houses of Reston, Virginia, each with its full leadership, practices, partners, clients, revenue, people, footprint and published contact route.

The roster is defensible rather than exhaustive. It holds the global majors, the European regionals, the United States public-sector tier, and a digital-native tier at verifiable scale. Two adjacent firms — Kyndryl, whose centre of gravity is managed infrastructure, and Concentrix, whose centre is customer experience operations — are included only with that framing stated in their entries. Deloitte appears here for its consulting and systems integration practice and is cross-referenced to the Accounting guide, where it also belongs. IBM Consulting and Hitachi Digital Services are framed as what they are: divisions within larger groups, not standalone firms.

The sector has been through a striking run of leadership change. Philippe Salle took the chair and then the chief executive's office at a restructured Atos; Raul Fernandez was made permanent at DXC and extended to 2028; Srini Pallia, a Wipro man since 1992, succeeded Thierry Delaporte; François Boulanger stepped up at CGI; Rajesh Krishnamurthy arrived at Sopra Steria in February 2026; Endre Rangnes took over a reshaped Tietoevry as it divested its managed-services business and prepared to become, simply, Tieto; Venu Lambu returned to lead LTIMindtree as it became LTM; Balazs Fejes succeeded EPAM's founder; and in Reston, James Reagan — a former Leidos finance chief — was made permanent chief executive of SAIC in February 2026. Every entry in this guide was checked against official reporting in October 2026, and every figure for revenue and people is dated. Where a firm does not publish a field — and the newly private firms, Perficient and Thoughtworks, publish far less than they did — the entry says so plainly rather than filling the space.

GAPS AND EXCLUSIONS, NAMED

Three kinds of gap are named rather than covered over. First, fields a firm does not publish: Perficient and Thoughtworks, both taken private in 2024–2025, no longer file public accounts, and their entries say so. Second, titles in transition at press time: Tech Mahindra's finance and operations titles, SAIC's October 2026 finance appointment, and Hitachi Digital Services' subsidiary chief executive are marked not verified in the entries themselves. Third, firms left out on purpose: General Dynamics Information Technology, Peraton and ManTech are held for a possible public-sector supplement; PwC, EY and KPMG belong to the Accounting guide; Avanade is covered through Accenture, its majority owner; GlobalLogic is noted inside the Hitachi entry and not double-counted; and the hyperscalers themselves — Microsoft, Amazon Web Services and Google Cloud — are platform owners, not integrators, however large their professional-services arms have become.
