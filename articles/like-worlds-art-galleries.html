AUCTIONS & COLLECTING · THE WORLDWIDE GUIDE

THE WORLD'S ART GALLERIES

By Michael de la Force · Editor-in-Chief, LIKE® Magazine · Auctions & Collecting · October 2026

Every auction record begins somewhere else: in a gallery, where an artist was first shown, first priced and first placed in a museum. The galleries are the primary market of the collecting world — and the least corporate institutions in it. Most have no chief executive, publish no revenue and answer to a founder whose name is on the door. This guide maps them as they actually are, titles and all.

The list covers 35 galleries in four sections: the eight global mega-galleries; the international majors of London, Paris, Brussels, Milan, Berlin, New York and Seoul; the independent majors of the United States and Berlin; and the worldwide houses of Latin America, the Middle East and Asia. For each gallery: its ownership as it stands in late 2026, its leadership under the exact titles the gallery publishes — Founder, President, Partner, Chairwoman — what its programme is, which artists it represents in summary, where its rooms stand, which fairs it shows at, and how a collector walks in. Where a gallery publishes no artist count, no staff figure and no revenue, the entry says "not published" rather than estimating one. This guide sits in Auctions & Collecting for a simple reason: the gallery trade is the collecting ecosystem's primary market, and these are the houses that make the artists, the prices and, in time, the auction records.

THE YEAR OF SUCCESSIONS AND ONE FAREWELL TO THE MODEL ITSELF

Begin with the changes, because 2025 and 2026 rearranged the trade. Marian Goodman Gallery completed the succession its founder planned: Marian Goodman died aged 97, and the gallery she founded in 1977 is run by its five partners, Philipp Kaiser — President and Partner — at their head, from a new Tribeca headquarters opened in October 2024 and a Los Angeles gallery opened the year before. Gladstone Gallery continues under its founder's name after Barbara Gladstone's death in June 2024. Hauser & Wirth changed chief executive in 2024, Mirella Roma succeeding Ewan Venters beneath presidents Iwan Wirth and Marc Payot. And then there is BLUM: Tim Blum, who with Jeff Poe introduced Yoshitomo Nara and Takashi Murakami to America, renamed the gallery after Poe's departure in 2023, opened in Tribeca in 2024 — and in July 2025 announced he would leave the traditional gallery model altogether, closing Los Angeles and Tokyo and ending the formal roster. "This is not about the market," he said. "This is about the system." His entry in this guide prints the transition in full, because a thirty-year major stepping off the fair-and-openings treadmill is itself the primary-market story of the year.

The expansion map moved too. Perrotin opened inside Claridge's in London in March 2025, its ninth city. Thaddaeus Ropac opened in Milan's Palazzo Belgioioso in 2025. David Zwirner completed its Los Angeles buildings in May 2024. Pace, the oldest American mega-gallery, announced in 2026 that it would reduce its roster and staff and return to its historic strengths — the founder's phrase was "back to the future." Against all of it stand the deliberate counter-models this guide is careful to include at full rank: Xavier Hufkens, a top-tier roster run from Brussels alone; Regen Projects, one Los Angeles building for thirty-five years; Kukje Gallery, one Seoul street and the Dansaekhwa canon; Galeria Luisa Strina, founded in São Paulo in 1974 and still founder-led; and The Third Line, the Dubai gallery that carried the Middle East's artists to the world's fairs from a warehouse in Al Quoz.

HOW TO READ THE GUIDE

Three cautions, printed once here and applied in every entry. First, titles: galleries are not corporations. Only three galleries in this guide publish a chief executive in the corporate form — Pace, Hauser & Wirth and Lisson — so every other Leadership field prints the titles the gallery itself uses, and says plainly that no CEO title is published. Inventing one would be a small lie told 32 times. Second, artists: a roster is summarised, never dumped. Where a gallery publishes a count — Hauser & Wirth's over 90 artists and estates, Zwirner's some 80, White Cube's over 60 — the count is printed with its source; where it does not, the entry names a short marquee set and says the total is not published. Names drawn from exhibition history are flagged "programme," because showing an artist and representing an artist are different things. Third, access: every gallery in this guide is free to walk into, and that is not a courtesy but the model — the exhibition is public, the purchase is by enquiry, and for the most demanded artists allocation is a discipline one founder, Thaddaeus Ropac, has stated in public: collectors who flip works at auction do not buy again. The waiting list, where it exists, is a relationship, not a form.
