FASHION · THE EDITORIAL

THE CREATIVE-DIRECTOR CHAIR

By Michael de la Force · Editor-in-Chief, LIKE® Magazine · Fashion · October 2026

Every generation of fashion believes its musical chairs are the fastest ever played. This time the claim is documented. Between the autumn of 2024 and the autumn of 2026, the creative direction of nearly every great house in Paris and Milan changed hands, was vacated, or passed — in one case — by inheritance. The chair itself has become the industry's most consequential seat.

Consider the roll of appointments. Matthieu Blazy left Bottega Veneta for Chanel. Jonathan Anderson left Loewe for Dior, taking women's, men's and haute couture together — the first designer since Christian Dior to hold all three. Demna left Balenciaga for Gucci, and Pierpaolo Piccioli took the Balenciaga chair Demna vacated. Maria Grazia Chiuri left Dior for Fendi, returning to her native Rome. Sarah Burton went to Givenchy; Michael Rider to Celine; Jack McCollough and Lazaro Hernandez crossed the Atlantic from Proenza Schouler to Anderson's old seat at Loewe. Louise Trotter took Bottega Veneta. Alessandro Michele, late of Gucci, resurfaced at Valentino. Haider Ackermann took Tom Ford. At Balmain, Antonin Tron succeeded Olivier Rousteing after fourteen years; at Dries Van Noten, Julian Klausner was promoted from within the studio to succeed the founder himself. At Hermès, Grace Wales Bonner was announced for menswear, succeeding Véronique Nichanian after a tenure of more than three decades, with a first collection scheduled for January 2027.

And then there is the transfer that describes the market in miniature. Pieter Mulier left Alaïa after its March 2026 show to become chief creative officer of Versace — a house that had itself just changed owners, passing from Capri Holdings to the Prada Group in December 2025, and had parted with Dario Vitale after a single collection. One designer's move thus left a Paris house studio-led and filled a Milan chair in the same season. The chair is no longer a post. It is a position in a league, with agents, clauses and transfer windows in all but name.

THE EMPTY CHAIRS

As this guide went to press, two of the greatest chairs in fashion stood empty at the same time. Anthony Vaccarello departed Saint Laurent on Oct. 9, 2026, after ten years; Kering, the house's owner, said a successor would be announced soon, and trade reporting indicated a successor had been selected but not named. A week later, on Oct. 15, Seán McGirr exited Alexander McQueen, also Kering-owned, with no successor announced and the house's shows moved permanently to London. Alaïa, under Richemont, is meanwhile led by its studio, with ready-to-wear design director Boaz Eli presenting the October 2026 show while the house confirms a creative director in its own time.

An empty chair is not necessarily a weak one. It is, however, an expensive one. A house without a named designer sells its archive, its icons and its boutique experience while the appointment is pending — products that carry a house for a season or two, not for a decade. The two vacancies also concentrate the mind of every group board watching: the successor generation is finite, its leading names are now almost all seated, and the price of the remaining chairs rises with each announcement.

THE FOUNDER'S ANSWER

Milan offered the counter-model. Giorgio Armani died on Sept. 4, 2025, having arranged his succession in advance with a lawyer's precision: a foundation, bylaws, and the design of his house left to the two people who had worked beside him longest — Leo Dell'Orco, now chairman of the board, for menswear, and Silvana Armani for womenswear. No search firm, no transfer fee, no debut collection judged against a stranger's archive. Whether continuity outsells novelty is the experiment the industry will watch for the rest of the decade.

Other houses have declined the chair altogether. Loro Piana, under LVMH, has no named artistic director and no runway show; its collections come from a design studio under a chief product officer, and its authority rests on fibre — cashmere, vicuña, the finest merino — rather than on a face. Moncler abolished the single chair in 2018 in favour of its Genius model of rotating collaborators; its chairman, Remo Ruffini, puts it plainly: "I don't have a creative director." And at Max Mara, Ian Griffiths has held the same chair since 1987 — thirty-nine years, longer than many of the new appointees have been alive. Permanence, it turns out, is also a strategy.

WHAT THE CHAIR PERFORMS ON

It is tempting to judge a creative director by the runway: ten minutes of clothes, twice a year, reviewed by breakfast. The economics say otherwise. The stage a creative director actually performs on is a network of boutiques, and the network is the profit engine of modern luxury. A flagship on the Rue Saint-Honoré or Via Montenapoleone sells the collection at full margin, gathers the client data, hosts the private appointments and carries the house's image in three dimensions. Wholesale and e-commerce extend reach; the boutique network compounds value.

The numbers frame every appointment in this guide. LVMH ended 2025 with 6,283 stores across its houses — the largest stage in luxury, and the context for Anderson's Dior and Chiuri's Fendi. Kering ended 2025 with 1,719 directly operated stores and a stated programme of discipline: Alexander McQueen's network is being halved by the end of 2026, which means its next creative director inherits a smaller, London-centred stage and a brief measured in profitability, not spectacle. The Prada Group ended 2025 with 843 directly operated stores — 423 of them Prada, 220 of them the newly acquired Versace — so Mulier's Versace debut performs on an estate that did not belong to the house's previous owner a year earlier. Hermès runs the opposite model: 294 stores, almost all directly operated, no discounting, and a waiting list treated as an asset; Wales Bonner inherits not a network to expand but a scarcity to protect. Even the turnarounds are boutique stories: Ferragamo, at 358 directly operated stores, is closing some seventy weaker doors while it rebuilds — a creative brief written in leases.

This is why the chair matters beyond fashion's own pages. A creative director's collection is reviewed in a week; the boutique estate it must fill is committed for a decade. Groups appoint designers the way they allocate capital — because that is, in the end, what an appointment is.

WHAT COMES NEXT

Prediction, labelled as such: the Saint Laurent and McQueen appointments, when they land, will complete the current generation, and the industry's centre of gravity will shift from hiring to judging — the first full-year results of the Blazy, Anderson, Demna and Chiuri eras arrive through 2027, and the boutique networks described in our guide will deliver the verdict in the only currency that settles arguments in this business. The houses that refused the chair — Loro Piana, Moncler, and the founder-led independents from Brunello Cucinelli to The Row — will be standing exactly where they stood, which is rather their point.
