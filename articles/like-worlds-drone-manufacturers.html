TECHNOLOGY · THE WORLDWIDE GUIDE

THE WORLD’S DRONE MANUFACTURERS

By Michael de la Force · Editor-in-Chief, LIKE® Magazine · Technology · October 2026

A camera that flies, a soldier that never sleeps, a blood bag crossing a country in thirteen minutes, a passenger aircraft with no pilot at all: the drone is not one industry but five, and this guide maps all of them. From DJI's Shenzhen Sky City to the Blue brothers' San Diego — the family lead that opens our README and resolves, on the evidence, into General Atomics and the Predator line — these are the twenty-five makers who define unmanned aviation in October 2026: who leads each house, with exact published titles; what it flies, platform by platform; whom it sells to, where the company itself says so; how large it is, with every figure dated and its basis named; and where a field could not be verified, the entry says so rather than guessing.

The list is organised in five sections: two consumer and prosumer houses; seven commercial and enterprise makers, from Paris surveying aircraft to Zipline's national medical logistics; five dual-use companies whose aircraft serve police, farmers and armies alike; ten defence houses, from the hand-launched Raven to the Reaper and the Kızılelma unmanned fighter; and one eVTOL maker, EHang, included honestly as context — a certificated passenger aircraft company, not a drone maker in the ordinary sense. Anduril is likewise framed as what it is: a defence technology company whose drone lines are one part of a larger whole. ModalAI closes the guide deliberately: a San Diego 'Blue UAS' company that is not the Blue family of the lead, and the entry says so, to keep the record straight.

CONSUMER & PROSUMER

1. DJI — SHENZHEN, CHINA

Founded 2006 · Consumer & prosumer

Leader: Frank Wang (Wang Tao), Founder and CEO — title as used by DJI in its own headquarters announcement

Leadership: Not published beyond the founder-CEO in official material consulted

Business: Consumer and prosumer camera drones; enterprise drones for public safety, inspection, surveying and agriculture; handheld gimbals and action cameras (Osmo); agricultural spraying systems; delivery drones (FlyCart).

Customers: Consumers and creators worldwide; enterprise categories published by DJI Enterprise: public safety, energy, construction, agriculture and surveying operators. Individual customers are not named as a list — not disclosed.

Scale: Private — no audited revenue published. Reported estimates of roughly US$11 billion annual sales (2025, reported, not company-published) and a reported ~70% share of the global consumer drone market are carried here as reported figures, not as DJI disclosures.

Platforms: Mini series; Air series; Mavic series (incl. Mavic 4 Pro); Avata 2; Neo; Flip; Inspire 3; Matrice 4 and Matrice 400 series; Agras T100/T50 agricultural aircraft; FlyCart 30/100 delivery aircraft; Phantom series (legacy).

Civil / defence mix: Civil-led and dual-use in effect: DJI states its products are for civilian use, while US authorities have restricted federal procurement of DJI aircraft (NDAA provisions and the DoD's 2022 designation of DJI as a Chinese military company, which DJI disputes). Posture as of Oct 2026: banned from US federal use; consumer and commercial sales continuing in many markets.

Footprint: Headquarters at DJI Sky City, Nanshan, Shenzhen (opened 2022); offices and subsidiaries in the United States, Germany, Japan, South Korea and Australia as published on dji.com.

Structure / ownership: Private, founder-led; Frank Wang is reported to hold a controlling stake. No listing; no audited public accounts.

Website: dji.com · Contact: email Not published; phone Not published. Via dji.com and authorised dealers worldwide; enterprise via DJI Enterprise partners. No published general email — DJI routes contact through its site and dealer network.

Founded in 2006 in Shenzhen by Frank Wang (Wang Tao), a Hong Kong University of Science and Technology graduate, from a dorm-room flight-controller project. The Phantom (2013) created the ready-to-fly camera-drone market; DJI then built the Mavic, Air, Mini, Avata, Inspire, Matrice and Agras lines and the Osmo camera family. The defining consumer-drone maker: the Phantom created the category and DJI's vertical integration — motors, gimbals, cameras, transmission (OcuSync) and flight software in-house — has kept it the default choice from weddings to wind-farm inspection. US market-access risk is the live issue: FCC covered-list proceedings in 2025-26 could restrict new DJI authorisations in the United States; the position was unresolved at press time and is recorded as such.

Sources: DJI official site and Sky City headquarters announcement (dji.com, Frank Wang quoted as Founder and CEO); company product pages, Oct 2026; reported market-share and sales estimates labelled as reported.

2. AUTEL ROBOTICS — SHENZHEN, CHINA

Founded 2014 · Consumer & prosumer

Leader: Not verified as a single current published title in official material consulted — stated plainly. (Randall Warnas was announced as CEO of the US-facing business in 2021 reporting; no current official global CEO page was verified for this edition.)

Leadership: Maxwell Lee and Li Hongjing named as key people in reference sources; no full official executive roster is published — stated plainly.

Business: Consumer and prosumer camera drones; enterprise drones for public safety, inspection and surveying; VTOL fixed-wing systems; drone nests and mesh-network operations.

Customers: Consumers; enterprise and public-safety operators as published categories. Named customers are not published as a list — not disclosed.

Scale: Private — no revenue published. No verified funding or valuation figure is carried; the cell states the gap rather than estimating.

Platforms: EVO Nano series; EVO Lite series; EVO II series (incl. Dual 640T); EVO Max 4T/4N; Dragonfish VTOL series; EVO Nest and Dragonfish Nest autonomous stations.

Civil / defence mix: Civil-led: consumer and enterprise positioning. Added to the US Commerce Department Entity List in 2024 reporting, restricting US exports to the company; Autel markets enterprise and public-safety variants, so the line is dual-use in effect though civil in positioning (as of Oct 2026).

Footprint: Headquarters in Shenzhen; R&D bases published in Seattle, Munich and Silicon Valley (company summit release).

Structure / ownership: Private; formerly a subsidiary of Autel Intelligent Technology, spun off in 2020.

Website: autelrobotics.com · Contact: email Not published; phone Not published. Via autelrobotics.com and authorised dealers; enterprise via regional partners. No published general email.

Founded in 2014 in Shenzhen as the drone arm of Autel Intelligent Technology (founded 2004, automotive diagnostics); spun out when the parent listed in 2020. Built the X-Star, then the EVO, EVO II, EVO Lite/ Nano and EVO Max families and the Dragonfish VTOL. The principal Shenzhen rival to DJI in folding camera drones, and the maker that pushed enterprise features — thermal, mesh networking, VTOL endurance — hardest into mid-size airframes. US Entity List status (2024) is recorded from reporting; readers should check current export-control status before procurement.

Sources: Autel Robotics official site and Global Partner Summit release (autelrobotics.com / company release, chairman and HQ/R&D statements); FCC filings for the Shenzhen address; DRONELIFE reporting on the 2021 US CEO appointment and 2024 Entity List addition.

DUAL-USE

3. SKYDIO — SAN MATEO, UNITED STATES

Founded 2014 · Dual-use

Leader: Adam Bry, Co-Founder and Chief Executive Officer

Leadership: Co-founders Bry, Bachrach and Donahoe lead; a fuller named executive roster is published in part on skydio.com — stated as consulted.

Business: Autonomous small drones for public safety (Drone as First Responder), infrastructure and utility inspection, surveying, and defence reconnaissance; autonomous Dock stations; Skydio Cloud fleet software.

Customers: As published by the company: more than 1,000 public-safety agencies, every branch of the US military, and customers in 29 allied nations (company statements, Apr 2026); enterprise categories: utilities, transportation, construction and energy.

Scale: Private — revenue not published as an audited figure; the company described 'hundreds of millions in annual revenue' in its April 2026 Series F statement (company-reported). Funding: US$110 million Series F (Apr 2026) at a reported US$4.4 billion valuation; cumulative funding reported at roughly US$950 million (reported figures, labelled as such). Announced April 2026: a US$3.5 billion, five-year US manufacturing expansion plan (company announcement).

Platforms: Skydio X10; Skydio X10D (defence variant); Skydio Dock (autonomous remote-operations station); Skydio X2 (legacy); Skydio 2/2+ (consumer, discontinued 2023).

Civil / defence mix: Explicitly dual-use and, since 2023, defence-and-enterprise only: a Blue UAS-cleared supplier to the US Department of Defense (X10D on the DIU Blue UAS list) whose largest civil market is public safety. China sanctioned Skydio in October 2024, cutting its battery supply — disclosed by the company at the time.

Footprint: Headquarters and manufacturing in the San Francisco Bay Area (San Mateo/Hayward, California); customers in 29 allied nations per the company.

Structure / ownership: Private, venture-backed (Andreessen Horowitz, Linse Capital, Accel, IVP, NVIDIA among published investors).

Website: skydio.com · Contact: email Not published; phone Not published. Via skydio.com (direct enterprise and government sales; public-safety programmes via the company). No published general email.

Founded in 2014 in California by Adam Bry, Abraham Bachrach and Matt Donahoe, MIT and Google X autonomy researchers. The R1 (2018) and Skydio 2 made obstacle-avoiding autonomy its signature; in 2023 Skydio exited the consumer market to concentrate on enterprise, public safety and defence. Autonomy first: Skydio's aircraft navigate by vision, not GPS, and its Drone as First Responder model — a docked X10 launched from a police station roof — is the clearest American answer to DJI in public safety. The October 2024 Chinese sanctions and battery-supply cut are the documented supply-chain lesson of the US drone industry; Skydio's 2026 domestic-manufacturing commitment is its response.

Sources: Skydio official site and Series F / manufacturing-expansion statements (skydio.com; DRONELIFE, Apr 2026); DIU Blue UAS cleared list; company statements on customers and valuation as reported.

COMMERCIAL & ENTERPRISE

4. PARROT — PARIS, FRANCE

Founded 1994 · Commercial & enterprise

Leader: Henri Seydoux, Chairman and Chief Executive Officer (Founder)

Leadership: Henri Seydoux leads; Parrot publishes executive information in its regulated filings on corporate.parrot.com rather than a C-suite roster — stated plainly.

Business: Professional micro-drones for inspection, surveying and photogrammetry; public-safety and defence small UAS; photogrammetry and agriculture software through subsidiaries and partners (Pix4D ecosystem heritage).

Customers: Published categories: inspection, surveying and construction professionals; public-safety agencies; defence customers including the US Army's Short Range Reconnaissance programme (ANAFI USA selected under the DIU Blue UAS effort) and French armed forces programmes (ANAFI UKR).

Scale: Listed — revenue published in regulated half-year and annual releases on corporate.parrot.com; readers are directed to the latest official release for the current figure rather than a stale number being carried here. Workforce of more than 500 published in company material.

Platforms: ANAFI Ai; ANAFI USA; ANAFI UKR; ANAFI Thermal (legacy); AR.Drone (legacy, 2010).

Civil / defence mix: Civil-led, defence-adopted: a professional civil range (inspection, mapping) with purpose-built government variants (ANAFI USA, made for US government users; ANAFI UKR for tactical reconnaissance) — dual-use in effect, civil in origin (as of Oct 2026).

Footprint: Headquarters in Paris; design and engineering in France and Switzerland; sales worldwide, with the United States a principal market (company releases).

Structure / ownership: Public — Euronext Paris: PARRO; founder Henri Seydoux is the controlling shareholder.

Website: parrot.com · Contact: email Not published as a general address — contact via parrot.com (press contacts are named in individual releases); phone +33 1 48 03 60 60 (as published in Parrot press material). Via parrot.com and enterprise resellers; government programmes via the company's defence channels. No published general email.

Founded in 1994 in Paris by Henri Seydoux, Christine de Tourvel and Jean-Pierre Talvard; listed on Euronext Paris in 2006. The AR.Drone (2010) opened the smartphone-controlled drone era; automotive and consumer lines were wound down and since 2017 Parrot has focused exclusively on professional drones — ANAFI, ANAFI USA, ANAFI Ai and ANAFI UKR. Europe's drone champion: the only Western maker to have fought DJI in consumer drones, survived, and rebuilt as a secure, sovereign professional platform — no data shared by default is its published security posture. Parrot's regulated filings (corporate.parrot.com) are the authoritative source for its current revenue; this guide names the route rather than carrying a dated figure out of context.

Sources: Parrot official site and press releases (parrot.com; corporate.parrot.com); Euronext Paris listing (PARRO); US DoD/DIU Short Range Reconnaissance and Blue UAS programme reporting.

DEFENSE UAS

5. AEROVIRONMENT — ARLINGTON, UNITED STATES

Founded 1971 · Defense UAS

Leader: Wahid Nawabi, Chairman, President and Chief Executive Officer (exact combined title as used in the company's results releases)

Leadership: Wahid Nawabi; Kevin P. McDonnell (SVP & CFO); Jonah Teeter-Balin, Vice President, Corporate Development and Investor Relations (named in releases).

Business: Uncrewed aircraft systems (small, medium and loitering); loitering munition systems; MacCready Works advanced programmes; following BlueHalo: space technologies, directed energy, cyber and electronic warfare.

Customers: The US Department of Defense (Army, Marine Corps, Air Force, SOCOM) as principal customer; allied militaries in more than 50 countries as published by the company; NASA (the Ingenuity Mars helicopter was co-developed with JPL).

Scale: Revenue US$820.6 million, FY2025 (year ended 30 April 2025), +14% year on year; net income US$43.6 million; record bookings US$1.2 billion (company results release, 24 June 2025). FY2026 figures reflect the BlueHalo consolidation — see avinc.com investor releases for the current year.

Platforms: Raven (RQ-11); Wasp AE; Puma (RQ-20) family incl. Puma LE; Switchblade 300 and Switchblade 600 loitering munitions; Jump 20; Vapor helicopter UAS; Snipe Nano UAS; Quantix Recon (legacy).

Civil / defence mix: Defence-led: the overwhelming majority of revenue is military. Civil touchpoints are scientific and exceptional — the Ingenuity Mars helicopter with NASA/JPL is the famous one (as of Oct 2026).

Footprint: Headquarters in Arlington, Virginia; principal design and manufacturing in Southern California (Simi Valley and Moorpark); operations serving 50+ allied nations.

Structure / ownership: Public — Nasdaq: AVAV.

Website: avinc.com · Contact: email Not published — investor and media contacts are named in individual releases; phone +1 703 418 2828 (as published in company profiles). Via avinc.com; government sales through US programme offices and Foreign Military Sales. No published general email.

Founded in 1971 by Paul B. MacCready Jr., inventor of the human-powered Gossamer Condor. AeroVironment's hand-launched Raven and Puma became the US military's standard small UAS; the Switchblade loitering munition followed. In 2025 it closed the acquisition of BlueHalo, broadening into space, directed energy and electronic warfare. The small-UAS pioneer: more hand-launched military drones in service worldwide than any rival, and — through Switchblade — the company that put the loitering munition into the infantry squad. The BlueHalo acquisition (closed 2025) materially changed the company's scale and mix; FY2025 figures above are the last pre-acquisition full year and are dated as such.

Sources: AeroVironment fiscal 2025 results release, 24 June 2025 (avinc.com / Business Wire); company leadership and product pages; Nasdaq: AVAV filings.

6. GENERAL ATOMICS AERONAUTICAL SYSTEMS (GA-ASI) — SAN DIEGO (POWAY), UNITED STATES

Founded 1993 · Defense UAS

Leader: Linden Blue, Chief Executive Officer, GA-ASI (title as used in GA-ASI releases, incl. July 2025 and September 2026)

Leadership: Linden Blue (CEO, GA-ASI); Neal Blue (Chairman & CEO, General Atomics). Further named executives appear in programme releases on ga-asi.com.

Business: Medium-altitude long-endurance (MALE) remotely piloted aircraft; collaborative combat aircraft; airborne sensors and Lynx radar; ground control stations; training and support services.

Customers: The US Air Force, US Army, US Marine Corps and US Customs and Border Protection (published operators); allied air forces including the United Kingdom (Protector), Australia, India (SeaGuardian/SkyGuardian sales), Japan Coast Guard and multiple European and Asian operators as published by GA-ASI.

Scale: Private — GA-ASI publishes no revenue. Parent General Atomics revenue was reported at US$3.2 billion (2024, reported figure for the whole group, incl. energy and electromagnetic systems — not a GA-ASI figure and labelled as such). GA-ASI states its aircraft have accumulated more than 9 million flight hours (company figure).

Platforms: MQ-9B SkyGuardian and SeaGuardian; MQ-9A Reaper (and UK Protector RG1); MQ-1C Gray Eagle and Gray Eagle STOL; MQ-20 Avenger; Mojave STOL demonstrator; Gambit family; YFQ-42A collaborative combat aircraft prototype; MQ-1 Predator (retired from US service 2018).

Civil / defence mix: Defence: a pure military and government line, with border-patrol and maritime-patrol government variants (Guardian/SeaGuardian for coast guards) the nearest approach to civil use (as of Oct 2026).

Footprint: Headquarters campus in Poway, California, with San Diego (Torrey Pines) parent headquarters; flight operations at Gray Butte and El Mirage, California, and Grand Forks, North Dakota; offices in Washington DC, the United Kingdom, Australia, India, Japan and the United Arab Emirates as published.

Structure / ownership: Private — General Atomics is owned by Neal Blue and Linden Blue (the Blue brothers); GA-ASI is its aeronautical subsidiary.

Website: ga-asi.com · Contact: email Not published — media contact is routed via ga-asi.com; phone Not published. Via ga-asi.com; sales are government-to-government (Foreign Military Sales) or direct commercial sales to governments. No published general email.

GA-ASI is the aeronautical arm of General Atomics, founded in 1955 as a General Dynamics division and bought in 1986 for a reported US$60 million by brothers Neal and Linden Blue — the 'Blue family' lead examined in this guide's README. GA-ASI (formalised in the early 1990s, building on the Amber and Gnat programmes) created the Predator, the aircraft that defined armed remotely piloted aviation; the Reaper, Gray Eagle, Avenger and Mojave followed, and its YFQ-42A is a US Air Force Collaborative Combat Aircraft prototype. The house the Blue brothers built into the defining name in long-endurance armed aviation: Predator to Reaper to the YFQ-42A uncrewed fighter prototype, all privately held from San Diego County. THE BLUE LEAD — RESOLVED: see the README. Michael's 'Blue family out of Del Mar, San Diego' is Neal and Linden Blue of General Atomics; Del Mar is the San Diego County coastal community adjoining the company's San Diego base. GA-ASI is therefore covered once, here, not duplicated as a separate 'Blue' entry.

Sources: GA-ASI official releases quoting Linden Blue as CEO (ga-asi.com / Access Newswire, Jul 2025 and Sep 2026); San Diego Reader cover reporting on the Blue brothers' 1986 purchase of General Atomics and the Predator's origins; General Atomics corporate history (ga.com); reference reporting on Neal Blue as Chairman & CEO of General Atomics.

7. BAYKAR — ISTANBUL, TURKEY

Founded 1984 · Defense UAS

Leader: Haluk Bayraktar, Chief Executive Officer (title as used by Forbes and company reporting; the company also styles him General Manager)

Leadership: Selçuk Bayraktar (Chairman & CTO); Haluk Bayraktar (CEO). No fuller official C-suite roster is published — stated plainly.

Business: Unmanned combat aerial vehicles; tactical and MALE UAS; C4I and AI software; loitering munitions (Kemankeş); through Piaggio Aerospace, manned aircraft manufacture; the Cezeri flying-car prototype programme.

Customers: The Turkish Armed Forces as anchor customer; published export customers in more than 30 countries for the TB2, including Ukraine, Poland (the first NATO/EU buyer), Azerbaijan, Qatar, Saudi Arabia and multiple African and Asian states, as announced by the company and Turkish export authorities.

Scale: Sales of about US$1.9-2.0 billion (2023, company-reported via Forbes and reference sources); exports of US$1.8 billion (2024, company-reported) — Turkey's largest defence exporter. Workforce of 8,000+ published in reference sources (2025). Production capacity stated by the company: 250 TB2 and 50 Akıncı per year.

Platforms: Bayraktar TB2; Bayraktar TB3 (naval); Bayraktar Akıncı; Bayraktar Kızılelma unmanned fighter (in development, deliveries targeted from 2026 in company statements); Bayraktar Mini UAV; Kemankeş mini cruise missile; Cezeri (prototype).

Civil / defence mix: Defence: an almost entirely military line; the Cezeri passenger prototype is a technology demonstrator, not a civil product (as of Oct 2026).

Footprint: Headquarters and the Özdemir Bayraktar National Technology Center in Istanbul (described by the company as the largest facility of its kind); a Ukraine facility (damaged in 2025, with rebuilding pledged by the company); Piaggio Aerospace in Italy; LBA Systems joint venture with Leonardo.

Structure / ownership: Private — owned by the Bayraktar family; Forbes reporting puts Selçuk Bayraktar at 52.5% and Haluk Bayraktar at 47.5% (reported).

Website: baykartech.com · Contact: email Not published — contact via baykartech.com; phone Not published. Via baykartech.com; sales are to governments, typically government-to-government. No published general email.

Founded in 1984 in Istanbul by Özdemir Bayraktar as Baykar Makina, a precision-machining and automotive-parts maker. From the mid-2000s, under sons Selçuk (engineering lead) and Haluk, it pivoted to unmanned aircraft; the Bayraktar TB2's combat record in Syria, Libya, Nagorno-Karabakh and Ukraine made Baykar Turkey's leading defence exporter. In 2024-25 it acquired Italy's Piaggio Aerospace and formed the LBA Systems joint venture with Leonardo. The TB2 effect: no drone since the Predator has changed more battlefields — or more defence-export league tables — than Baykar's, and it did so from a family-owned machining company. Piaggio Aerospace acquisition and the Leonardo LBA Systems venture (2025) mark Baykar's move into European industrial partnerships; both are recorded from company announcements.

Sources: Baykar official site and press releases (baykartech.com); Forbes profiles of Haluk and Selçuk Bayraktar (ownership, titles, 2023 sales, 2024 exports); company statements on production capacity and Kızılelma timing.

8. ANDURIL INDUSTRIES — COSTA MESA, UNITED STATES

Founded 2017 · Defense UAS

Leader: Brian Schimpf, Co-Founder and Chief Executive Officer

Leadership: Brian Schimpf (CEO); Palmer Luckey (Founder); Trae Stephens (Executive Chairman); Matt Grimm (COO); Christian Brose (Chief Strategy Officer).

Business: Defence technology company — framed honestly: drones are one part. Lattice AI command-and-control software; autonomous aircraft and collaborative combat aircraft; counter-UAS interceptors; loitering munitions and cruise missiles (Barracuda); autonomous undersea vehicles; surveillance towers; soldier systems (IVAS programme taken over from Microsoft in 2025).

Customers: The US Department of Defense (Air Force CCA programme, Army, Marine Corps, SOCOM, Customs and Border Protection) as published; the United Kingdom Ministry of Defence and Australian Defence Force as published customers.

Scale: Private — revenue reported at about US$2.2 billion (2025, reported); valuation US$61 billion after a US$5 billion Series H (May 2026, company-reported via Bloomberg); an October 2026 employee tender implied about US$90 billion (reported — a tender price, not a funding valuation, and labelled as such). Workforce reported at 7,000-8,000 (2026, reported).

Platforms: Fury (YFQ-44A) collaborative combat aircraft; Ghost and Ghost-X autonomous helicopters; ALTIUS-600/700 tube-launched aircraft; Roadrunner reusable VTOL interceptor; Anvil counter-UAS interceptor; Bolt loitering munition; Barracuda-100/250/500 (missiles, not drones — included in company materials).

Civil / defence mix: Defence: Anduril sells to militaries and border/security agencies only; it publishes no civil product line (as of Oct 2026).

Footprint: Headquarters in Costa Mesa, California; Arsenal-1 under construction near Columbus, Ohio; offices in Washington DC, Seattle, Atlanta, London (United Kingdom) and Sydney (Australia), with staff in 16 countries per its 2026 tender reporting.

Structure / ownership: Private, venture-backed (Founders Fund, Andreessen Horowitz, Thrive Capital among published investors).

Website: anduril.com · Contact: email Not published — contact via anduril.com; phone Not published. Via anduril.com; sales to governments and defence customers. No published general email.

Founded in 2017 in Costa Mesa, California, by Palmer Luckey with Trae Stephens, Brian Schimpf, Matt Grimm and Joe Chen. Anduril inverted the defence model — build with private capital, sell finished systems — around the Lattice software platform, then expanded into autonomous aircraft (Ghost, Fury), interceptors (Roadrunner, Anvil), tube-launched effects (ALTIUS), undersea vehicles (Dive-LD, acquired) and the Arsenal-1 mass-manufacturing plant in Ohio. The software-first prime: Lattice is the product and the aircraft are its peripherals — the model that took a nine-year-old start-up to a Collaborative Combat Aircraft contract alongside General Atomics. Anduril is included as a drone manufacturer because Fury, Ghost, ALTIUS, Roadrunner and Bolt are fielded unmanned aircraft — but readers should note the company's own framing as a broader defence technology company, which this entry preserves.

Sources: Anduril official site and product pages (anduril.com); Series H reporting (Bloomberg, May 2026) and October 2026 tender reporting; US Air Force CCA programme announcements (2024).

The AI pilot as the product: Shield AI's wager is that autonomy, not airframes, is the scarce asset — priced by investors in 2026 accordingly. Shield AI is a San Diego company — the same county as the Blue family's General Atomics — but no connection between the two beyond geography is suggested or found.

10. TELEDYNE FLIR DEFENSE — WILSONVILLE / THOUSAND OAKS, UNITED STATES
