MUSEUMS · THE ESSAY

THE DIRECTORS: A NEW GENERATION OF MUSEUM LEADERS AS CULTURAL STATESMEN

By Michael de la Force · Editor-in-Chief, LIKE® Magazine · Museums · October 2026

In the space of two years, a dozen of the world's great museum chairs have changed hands — in Paris, London, New York, Bilbao, Vienna, Moscow, Boston, Philadelphia, Abu Dhabi and Seoul. The men and women who now hold them negotiate with presidents and ministers, move treasures between nations underwritten by governments, and receive the public in numbers that rival national airlines. The museum director has become a cultural statesman, and the loan exhibition is his instrument of state.

Consider the season just passed. At the Louvre, Christophe Leribault took office in February 2026 after the theft of the French crown jewels from the Galerie d'Apollon the previous October — a burglary that became, within four months, a change of national museum leadership accepted at the Élysée itself, and a renovation and security programme of some $820 million. At the Museum of Modern Art in New York, Christophe Cherix succeeded Glenn D. Lowry, who had held the directorship for thirty years; at the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, Pierre Terjanian arrived from the Metropolitan Museum of Art; at the Philadelphia Museum of Art, the board reached back to the Met's former president, Daniel H. Weiss. In Bilbao, Miren Arzalluz succeeded a director of thirty-two years; in Vienna, Jonathan Fine became the first American to lead the Habsburg imperial collections; in Moscow, the directorships of the Tretyakov Gallery and the Pushkin Museum changed occupants in a single January week. At Tate, the transition is printed in layers: Maria Balshaw stepped down in December 2025, Karin Hindsbo serves as interim director, and Jessica Morgan takes office on 1 January 2027. In Seoul, the directorship of the National Museum of Korea fell vacant on 21 October 2026, when You Hong-june resigned over a misattributed calligraphy scroll — a reminder that these offices now carry a statesman's exposure as well as a scholar's brief.

What travels between their institutions is the substance of the job. In September 2026 the British Museum opened its galleries to the Bayeux Tapestry, lent by France and displayed in Britain for the first time in some one thousand years. A loan of that rank is not arranged between registrars alone: the United Kingdom government underwrote it with a guarantee of £800 million, and Nicholas Cullinan, the British Museum's director, received what amounts to an instrument of Franco-British relations — seventy metres of embroidered history. In Paris, the Musée du Quai Branly – Jacques Chirac, under its president Emmanuel Kasarhérou, spent the winter hosting Dragons, eighty-three objects lent by the National Palace Museum in Taipei under its director Hsiao Tsung-huang — a loan read on three continents as carefully as any communiqué. In Athens, the Acropolis Museum has hosted the British Museum's Meidias Hydria, a vase returning to the shadow of the Parthenon by agreement rather than by claim: diplomacy conducted object by object, at walking pace, in public.

The money that follows is not incidental. The Louvre received 9,046,000 visitors in 2025; the Vatican Museums 6,933,822; the National Museum of Korea 6,507,483, up some 72 per cent in a single year on the strength of Korean screen culture. The Grand Egyptian Museum, opened by Ahmed Ghoneim's authority in November 2025 before an assembly of heads of state, counted about four million visitors in its first ten months. A blockbuster loan season fills hotels and restaurants around the museum that hosts it, and empties none of the museum that lends: the lender's name travels with the object, printed in another nation's capital. Two directors have begun to buy as statesmen buy. In 2016 the Rijksmuseum under Taco Dibbits and the Louvre jointly acquired Rembrandt's pendant portraits of Marten Soolmans and Oopjen Coppit for €160 million, the portraits alternating between Amsterdam and Paris — two nations sharing one masterpiece by treaty-like arrangement rather than contesting it. In 2026 the Rijksmuseum and the Mauritshuis, under Martine Gosselink, made their first joint purchase, a Hendrick Avercamp winter scene. In Washington, the trustee Mitchell P. Rales endowed the National Gallery of Art's Across the Nation lending programme with $116 million in May 2026, institutionalising the loan itself as the museum's public mission under director Kaywin Feldman.

The statesman's other problem is success at home. At the Prado, Miguel Falomir argued in January 2026 that a museum's success can collapse it, and defended a deliberate ceiling on crowds in front of Las Meninas — a curator arguing, in effect, that scarcity is a form of stewardship. At the Centre Pompidou, Laurent Le Bon faces the opposite condition: the Beaubourg building closed in September 2025 and reopens in 2030, and Europe's largest modern collection is being administered for five years as a travelling one, placed by partnership in the Grand Palais, the Louvre and institutions abroad. In Los Angeles, Michael Govan opened the David Geffen Galleries in April 2026, completing the campus he has spent twenty years assembling. In Abu Dhabi, Souraya Noujaim took up the directorship of Louvre Abu Dhabi in October 2026 — a museum that exists because two governments signed an agreement in 2007 licensing the Louvre's name until 2047, and whose founding director, Manuel Rabaté, departed this year to lead a museum in New Delhi. The circuit these directors travel is now a single world circuit, and its postings are filled the way embassies are filled: from a small, known, international cadre.

Prediction — labelled as such: the next decade's defining museum agreements will be written less by curators than by guarantors. As values climb past what any commercial insurer will carry, the state guarantee behind the Bayeux loan will become the standard currency of the great exhibition, and a director's fluency with finance ministries will count for as much as fluency with the collection. A further prediction: at least one of the directorships in this guide that stands vacant or interim at press time will be filled from outside the museum profession altogether — from diplomacy, finance or the media — and the appointment will be defended, correctly, on the ground that the job has already changed.

What does not change is the ground of the statesman's authority. It is not the blockbuster, the guarantee or the visitor count. It is the collection held in trust — eight million objects in Bloomsbury, 854 in the Mauritshuis, a jade cabbage in Taipei that a nation queues to see — and the director's first duty remains the oldest one: to hand it on intact, better understood, to a successor not yet named. The new generation knows the world is watching. The collections have always known it.

The directors, visitor figures and loan arrangements in this essay are drawn from LIKE® Magazine's guide, The World's Great Museums, compiled October 2026 from official museum sources and reputable press, where each entry carries its sources.
