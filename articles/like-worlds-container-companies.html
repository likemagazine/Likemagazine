TRANSPORTATION · THE WORLDWIDE GUIDE

THE WORLD'S CONTAINER COMPANIES

By Michael de la Force · Editor-in-Chief, LIKE® Magazine · Transportation · October 2026

The shipping lines in the companion guide own surprisingly few of the boxes they carry. Roughly half the world's container fleet belongs to the companies in this guide: the lessors, who buy boxes by the hundred thousand and rent them to the lines, and behind them the manufacturers — four factories groups, all Chinese, who build nearly every box on earth.

It is the most concentrated supply chain in world trade, and in May 2026 it produced the industry's largest legal story: a US indictment alleging that the four biggest makers fixed prices and capped production through the pandemic shortage. The allegations are unproven and are disputed by the companies; they are recorded, factually and without embellishment, in the entries they touch — CIMC, Singamas, CXIC and Dong Fang — because they concern the named leadership of those entries.

One name in this guide no longer trades. Sea Containers, which invented container leasing in 1965, is carried as a marked heritage entry: its leasing fleet descends, by a chain this guide traces link by link, into the Textainer–Seaco combination announced in December 2025, and its Orient-Express arm lives on as LVMH's Belmond.

HERITAGE — THE HOUSE THAT INVENTED THE BUSINESS

16. SEA CONTAINERS — HERITAGE (DEFUNCT 2006–09)

United Kingdom — London (and New York) · Founded 1965 — Chapter 11 October 2006; wound down thereafter

Category: HERITAGE ENTRY — former container lessor and transport group

Region: Europe / North America

HQ Country: United Kingdom

Founded: 1965 — Chapter 11 October 2006; wound down thereafter

History: HERITAGE ENTRY, CLEARLY MARKED: Sea Containers was founded in London in 1965 by James B. Sherwood, an American-born former US Navy officer, to lease the new invention — the standard shipping container — to the lines that could not yet afford to own it. The leasing arm (SeaCo) became one of the world's largest container fleets; the group expanded into ferries (Sealink, Hoverspeed, the Isle of Man Steam Packet), Scandinavian ferry stakes, and luxury travel, buying the Venice Simplon-Orient-Express train in 1982 and building Orient-Express Hotels around it. GE Capital bought the container-leasing business in 1998 (GE SeaCo). Burdened by ferry losses and debt, Sea Containers filed for Chapter 11 in October 2006; the remaining businesses were sold or wound down, and the group disappeared by the end of the decade.

Ownership / Parent: Defunct. Founder-led (James Sherwood) in its great years

Leadership: No current leadership — the company no longer exists. Founder and defining chief executive: James B. Sherwood (1933–2020)

Full C-Suite / Senior Leadership: Not applicable — defunct

Specialties: Was: container leasing at world scale; cross-Channel and Irish Sea ferries; luxury rail and hotels (Orient-Express)

Fleet / Capacity (dated): At its height the SeaCo leasing fleet ranked among the world's two or three largest; sold to GE Capital in 1998

Scale (dated): A FTSE-era group spanning leasing, ferries and hotels; Chapter 11 filing October 2006

Offices / Footprint: None — wound down. Its London and New York offices are closed

Website: None (retired)

Contact Email: Not applicable

Phone: Not applicable

HQ Address: Formerly Sea Containers House, Upper Thames Street, London, United Kingdom

Distinctiveness: The inventor of the industry's business model: before Sea Containers, lines owned their boxes; Sherwood taught them to rent

Access / Entry: Not applicable — but its world survives, in two places this guide can point to precisely

Notes: Where it went: (1) The leasing lineage — SeaCo passed to GE Capital (1998), then to HNA/Bohai (2011), and in December 2025 Textainer announced its acquisition of Seaco — the direct descendant of Sherwood's leasing fleet now joins the world's largest lessor (see the Seaco entry above). (2) The Orient-Express arm — floated as Orient-Express Hotels, renamed Belmond in 2014, and acquired by LVMH in 2019 for about US$2.6 billion; the Venice Simplon-Orient-Express still runs under Belmond today. METHOD, GAPS AND EXCLUSIONS

Fleet figures for lessors are owned-plus-managed fleets as published by the companies or, where a company publishes no single figure (Florens, Beacon, SeaCube, Touax), industry estimates labelled as estimates in the entry itself. CEU (cost-equivalent units) is used only where the companies themselves announce on that basis (the Textainer–Seaco combination).

Excluded by design: small regional lessors and the container-trading/resale trade (covered inside the big lessors' entries); container-ship tonnage providers (the Seaspan/Atlas and Danaos model — they lease ships, not boxes, and belong to a future tonnage guide); and portable-storage rental fleets (WillScot and peers), a different industry that rents the same steel for building sites.
