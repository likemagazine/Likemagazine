It Ain’t Over: A Bronx Comeback and the Neighborhood Across the River

Held hitless for seven innings by Seattle’s Bryan Woo, the Yankees rallied to beat the Mariners 6–5 in 10 innings on July 10, 2025. LIKE® Sports on the comeback, the ballpark across 161st Street, a 1967 LIKE cover and the Washington Heights families who have always made the trip.

By LIKE® Sports · Photos by Michael de la Force / LIKE Magazine · Sports

For seven innings on Thursday night, July 10, 2025, the Bronx was the quietest place in New York. Seattle’s Bryan Woo held the Yankees without a hit, the Mariners led 5–0, and a crowd of 41,241 had little to do but watch the light go down behind the frieze. Then the Yankees hit back. They scored three in the eighth and two in the ninth, and in the 10th Anthony Volpe slid home on Aaron Judge’s sacrifice fly to win it, 6–5.

Yogi Berra, who caught for the Yankees across the street from here, liked to say it ain’t over till it’s over. On this night his ballclub proved it again.

Seven innings of nothing

Woo, an All-Star that summer, walked the first two batters he faced and then shut the door. No other Yankee reached base until the eighth. Cole Young singled home a run in the second and Miles Mastrobuoni singled home another in the fourth, both off Marcus Stroman, who gave up two runs in five innings. In the seventh, Jorge Polanco hit a three-run home run off Clayton Beeter, and the game looked settled.

It was not. Jazz Chisholm Jr. opened the bottom of the eighth with a ground single to right, the first Yankees hit of the night. Ben Rice followed with a single to center that sent Chisholm to third, and Austin Wells lifted a sacrifice fly to left. That ended Woo’s night after a career-high 103 pitches and a career-best seven and a third innings.

With a runner on and two out, manager Aaron Boone sent Giancarlo Stanton to pinch-hit against Matt Brash. Stanton had been 5-for-48 as a pinch-hitter in his career, with no home runs. He hit a 97.6 mph sinker for a two-run home run, the first pinch-hit homer of his career, and it was 5–3. “No lead is safe,” Stanton said afterward.

Down to the last strike

Seattle turned to its All-Star closer, Andrés Muñoz, in the ninth. Trent Grisham and Cody Bellinger singled, and Rice walked to load the bases with two out. Wells worked the count full, one strike from the end, and lined a single to right that scored both runners. It was 5–5, and it was Muñoz’s sixth blown save of the season.

Devin Williams kept Seattle off the board in the top of the 10th. In the bottom half Volpe began the inning at second base as the automatic runner. A bunt popped up, Paul Goldschmidt was walked intentionally as a pinch-hitter, and Grisham walked to send Volpe to third. Judge then lifted a fly to medium-depth center field off Gabe Speier. Julio Rodríguez made a strong throw, but Volpe slid around catcher Cal Raleigh’s tag and slapped the plate with his hand. The Mariners challenged, the call stood, and Frank Sinatra sang over the stadium speakers.

“I’m glad Volpe’s fast,” Judge said.

ESPN Research found that no team had come back to win after being held hitless through seven innings and trailing by five or more since 1977, a span of 236 such games. It was the Yankees’ first win when trailing by at least five runs in the eighth inning or later since June 29, 2016, against Texas. It was also their third walk-off win of the season and their fourth straight victory, and it finished a three-game sweep of Seattle that began with wins of 10–3 and 9–6.

The house across 161st Street

Comebacks are a house specialty in this building. The present Yankee Stadium played its first regular-season game on April 16, 2009, across 161st Street from the original park, which opened on April 18, 1923. The Associated Press put the new ballpark’s price at $1.5 billion when it opened, and its architects brought the old park’s white frieze back to crown the upper deck. In its first October, the Yankees won the 2009 World Series there. On September 25, 2014, Derek Jeter played his last game in the Bronx and won it with a walk-off single, 6–5 over Baltimore. Eleven summers later Volpe’s slide finished another 6–5 night.

Batter up, 1967

LIKE® has been covering New York sports since the sixties, and the proof is in our archive. The April 1967 Election Issue, priced at 40 cents, put two Yankees on the cover. Joe DiMaggio, in a dark suit and dotted tie, shakes hands with Mickey Mantle, in pinstripes, under the upper deck. “BATTER UP!” reads the cover line, as DiMaggio welcomes Mantle to the new season. Then it sends readers on to the night that followed: after the game, Toots Shor’s, the Barberry Room, El Morocco.

The photograph is believed to date from Mickey Mantle Day, September 18, 1965, when the Yankees honored Mantle before his 2,000th game with the club. DiMaggio introduced him to the crowd that afternoon. “I think just to have the greatest baseball player I ever saw introduce me is tribute enough for me in one day,” Mantle said. At his request, the proceeds from the day went to research on Hodgkin’s disease.

It is a portrait of an era in a single handshake. DiMaggio had left center field after the 1951 season, the quiet, exact star who once told a packed stadium, on his day in October 1949, “I want to thank the Good Lord for making me a Yankee.” Mantle, raised in Commerce, Oklahoma, had taken over center field and carried the franchise through the 1950s and early 1960s. By 1967, worn down by injuries, he was moving to first base for his final seasons. That year the club also adopted a new anthem, “Here Come the Yankees,” written by Lou Stallman and Bob Bundin.

A year later Simon & Garfunkel would ask, in “Mrs. Robinson,” where Joe DiMaggio had gone. The cover already had an answer: he was at the ballpark, welcoming the next man. Mantle retired in 1969. In 1978 he told a UPI reporter, borrowing an old quip, “If I knew I was gonna live this long, I’d have taken better care of myself.”

Across the Harlem River

The ballpark has always drawn from the neighborhood across the water. Washington Heights, at the top of Manhattan, looks across the Harlem River toward the Bronx, and generations of its families have made the short trip to the stadium.

One of them was a teenage refugee named Henry Kissinger. Born in Fürth, Germany, in 1923, he arrived in New York with his family in 1938, at 15, and settled in Washington Heights among other German Jewish refugees. He attended George Washington High School, then worked days in a shaving-brush factory and finished his studies at night. He also found the Yankees. “At that time, I could only afford bleacher seats,” he later told Yankees Magazine. “From the bleachers at Yankee Stadium, I had a great view of Joe DiMaggio playing centerfield.”

The boy in the bleachers became secretary of state, and in later years he was a frequent guest of the Steinbrenner family at the stadium. When he died on November 29, 2023, at 100, the Yankees called him “a lifelong friend of the Yankees organization.” LIKE photographed him in New York in a Yankees satin jacket, pen in hand.

George Washington High has a baseball line of its own. Rod Carew came to Washington Heights from Panama in 1962, enrolled at the school, and worked as a grocery clerk while he learned English. He did not play high school ball. He played sandlot ball instead, with a team called the Cavaliers that used the fields of Macombs Dam Park, in the shadow of the old Yankee Stadium. Minnesota scouts noticed him there, the Twins gave him a tryout at the stadium, and he signed in 1964. He reached the Hall of Fame with 3,053 hits and seven batting titles. The present Yankee Stadium stands where some of those fields once were.

Over the 1970s and 1980s, Washington Heights became the heart of Dominican New York, and baseball came with it. In 1991 every player on the George Washington varsity was Dominican, and the team’s best hitter was Manny Ramírez. His family had moved to the neighborhood from Santo Domingo in 1985. That June, Cleveland took him with the 13th pick of the draft, and he went on to a career of 555 home runs. Alex Rodriguez was born in New York in 1975 to Dominican parents who ran a shoe store in Washington Heights, and in 2009 he helped win the first World Series in the new ballpark.

It ain’t over

By the time Volpe stood up from the plate, the sunset over the frieze was long gone. The Yankees had come from nowhere: no hits for seven innings, a five-run deficit, two outs and a full count in the ninth. Their fans went home across the river and all over the city with one more comeback to tell.

“Baseball is funny like that,” Boone said. In the Bronx it usually is.
