TRANSPORTATION · THE WORLDWIDE GUIDE

THE WORLD'S SHIPPING LINES

By Michael de la Force · Editor-in-Chief, LIKE® Magazine · Transportation · October 2026

Ninety per cent of everything you own arrived by sea, and most of it arrived in a steel box. This guide maps the companies that carry those boxes: the 22 operating container lines of world scale, ranked on the industry's common measure — operated fleet capacity in TEU (twenty-foot equivalent units), owned and chartered ships together, per Alphaliner, September 2026 — plus one heritage entry, clearly marked, for the line whose collapse taught the industry its limits.

The field is concentrated to a degree no antitrust authority in any other industry would tolerate: the top ten carriers control about 85 per cent of world capacity, and they cooperate in three alliances — Gemini, the Ocean Alliance and the Premier Alliance — that set the effective shape of world trade. Each entry names the carrier's leadership with exact titles, its alliance, its principal trades and its published contacts.

A note on basis: fleet figures are operated capacity, not cargo carried, and they move daily; each is dated. Where a carrier's executive team is not published in English-language sources to this guide's standard, the cell says so rather than guess.

THE GLOBAL MAJORS

1. MSC MEDITERRANEAN SHIPPING COMPANY

Switzerland — Geneva · Founded 1970

Category: Container liner (private)

Region: Europe

HQ Country: Switzerland

Founded: 1970

History: Founded in Naples in 1970 by Captain Gianluigi Aponte with one second-hand vessel; grew by acquisition and chartering into the world's largest container line, overtaking Maersk in operated capacity in January 2022. Still wholly family-owned.

Ownership / Parent: Private — Aponte family (founder Gianluigi Aponte and family)

Leadership: Gianluigi Aponte — Founder and Group Chairman; Søren Toft — Chief Executive Officer (since December 2020)

Full C-Suite / Senior Leadership: MSC is privately held and does not publish a full C-suite on the Western listed-company model; the named executive leadership is the Chairman and the CEO, with the wider Aponte family in senior roles. Stated plainly rather than reconstructed from directories.

Specialties: Global container liner shipping on every major trade; the largest newbuilding programme in the industry; terminals via Terminal Investment Limited (TiL); wider group spans cruises (MSC Cruises, Explora Journeys), ferries (GNV), air cargo (MSC Air Cargo) and logistics (MEDLOG)

Fleet (vessels and TEU, dated): Operated fleet about 7,396,906 TEU, ranked No. 1 worldwide — Operated fleet (owned + chartered), Alphaliner Top 100 via Logistics Management, early September 2026. The largest container orderbook in the industry (about 3.39 million TEU on order per Alphaliner, October 2026)

Alliance / Cooperation: Independent — operates its own network outside the alliance system since the 2M split with Maersk (2025); slot cooperation on selected trades

Clients / Customers: Shippers, freight forwarders and NVOCCs on all major trades; among the largest carriers for most global forwarders

Scale (dated): World's largest container fleet: ~7.40 million TEU operated, 21.5% world share (Alphaliner, September 2026); group revenue not published (private)

Offices / Footprint: Agencies in 155 countries; about 675 offices worldwide (MSC-published figures)

Website: msc.com

Contact Email: Not published (contact via the local agency directory at msc.com)

Phone: +41 22 703 8888

HQ Address: 12–14 Chemin Rieu, 1208 Geneva, Switzerland

Distinctiveness: The only carrier of its scale that is still a family company — no listing, no alliance, and a founder still in the chairman's office

Access / Entry: Book via local MSC agency (directory on msc.com), the myMSC digital platform, or any major freight forwarder

Notes: Fleet figures are operated capacity (owned plus chartered-in tonnage), the standard Alphaliner basis used throughout this guide

Sources: Alphaliner Top 100 (September–October 2026); msc.com corporate pages; MSC press releases

2. A.P. MOLLER – MAERSK (MAERSK LINE)

Denmark — Copenhagen · Founded 1904

Category: Container liner and integrated logistics (listed)

Fleet (vessels and TEU, dated): Operated fleet about 4,749,620 TEU, ranked No. 2 — Operated fleet (owned + chartered), Alphaliner Top 100 via Logistics Management, early September 2026

Alliance / Cooperation: Gemini Cooperation with Hapag-Lloyd (from 1 February 2025), built around hub-and-shuttle networks and a 90% schedule-reliability ambition

Clients / Customers: Among the largest customers of any carrier: global retailers, automotive and consumer majors on long-term contracts; also the largest carrier for many national export trades

23. HANJIN SHIPPING — HERITAGE (DEFUNCT 2017)

South Korea — Seoul · Founded 1977 — bankrupt 17 February 2017

Category: HERITAGE ENTRY — former container liner

Region: Asia

HQ Country: South Korea

Founded: 1977 — bankrupt 17 February 2017

History: HERITAGE ENTRY, CLEARLY MARKED: Hanjin Shipping was founded in 1977 as the container arm of the Hanjin Group (Korean Air's parent) and grew into South Korea's largest carrier and the world's seventh-largest container line, operating about 98 vessels and some 617,000 TEU at its peak. Crushed by the post-2008 freight depression and its own charter commitments, it filed for court receivership on 31 August 2016 — the largest bankruptcy in container-shipping history. Ships were arrested or turned away from ports worldwide; the Seoul court declared bankruptcy on 17 February 2017 and the company was liquidated.

Ownership / Parent: Defunct. Formerly Hanjin Group (Chairman Cho Yang-ho)

Leadership: No current leadership — the company no longer exists. Final chairman: Cho Yang-ho of Hanjin Group

Full C-Suite / Senior Leadership: Not applicable — defunct

Specialties: Was: a top-seven global container line, member of the CKYHE alliance, with particular strength on trans-Pacific and Asia–Europe trades

Fleet (vessels and TEU, dated): At collapse (2016): about 617,000 TEU operated, ranked No. 7 in the world — the largest liner failure on record

Alliance / Cooperation: Was a member of the CKYHE Alliance (COSCO, K Line, Yang Ming, Hanjin, Evergreen)

Clients / Customers: Its customer book dispersed to rivals in 2016–17; its Asia–North America service network was bought out of receivership by SM Line, a new carrier created by the SM Group for the purpose

Scale (dated): Peak revenue about KRW 10 trillion (2013); liquidation followed the February 2017 bankruptcy declaration

Offices / Footprint: None — liquidated. Its Seoul HQ era is closed

Website: None (domain retired)

Contact Email: Not applicable

Phone: Not applicable

HQ Address: Formerly Hanjin Group Building, Seoul, South Korea

Distinctiveness: The industry's defining failure: Hanjin proved a top-ten carrier could simply stop, with 400,000+ containers stranded at sea and in ports

Access / Entry: Not applicable — see HMM (the state-backed heir of its national role) and SM Line (buyer of its trans-Pacific network)

Notes: Where its network went: HMM absorbed Hanjin's role as Korean national carrier and many of its people and trades; SM Line acquired its Asia–US services and the Hanjin brand rights for the Pacific trades; its ships were sold or returned to owners (many to Maersk, MSC and Seaspan) and its terminals were sold piecemeal (the Long Beach terminal stake went to MSC's TiL). Michael named Hanjin as his exemplar for this list; it is carried as heritage because a world shipping guide without it would misstate the industry's history

Sources: Seoul Central District Court bankruptcy declaration (17 February 2017); Alphaliner historical rankings; contemporary reporting by Lloyd's List, The Loadstar and the Wall Street Journal

METHOD, GAPS AND EXCLUSIONS

Excluded by design: pure bulk, tanker and gas carriers (several appear inside their groups' entries — Grimaldi is included on Alphaliner's container basis, with its ro-ro centre of gravity stated); DP World's shipping arm (Unifeeder and P&O Ferrymasters), which Alphaliner ranks in the top twenty but which is a feeder and short-sea business inside a ports group — it belongs to the ports story told in the companion Port Security guide; and the long tail of sub-60,000 TEU regional operators.

Hanjin Shipping is carried as a marked heritage entry at Michael's direction and on the merits: it was the world's seventh-largest line within a decade of this guide's publication, and its 2016–17 collapse remains the industry's defining event. Its network's heirs — HMM and SM Line — are traced inside the entry.
