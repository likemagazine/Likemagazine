PHANTOM HUMMINGBIRD: ROLLS-ROYCE TAKES WING IN ABALONE AND PEARL

For a Phantom Extended Private Commission curated by Private Office Dubai, Rolls-Royce sets abalone shell into its marquetry for the very first time, and lets a hummingbird loose in the cabin.

By Michael de la Force · Editor-in-Chief, LIKE® Magazine · October 9, 2026 · LIKE® Craftsmanship · Automotive Features · Style · Rolls-Royce PressClub coverage

Some love letters are written on paper. This one comes with a Starlight Headliner. Phantom Hummingbird is a Phantom Extended Private Commission conceived as a tribute to a client's wife and curated by Private Office Dubai. Its muse is a tiny bird that steals every scene: the hummingbird, a symbol of joy, of resilience, and of finding beauty in every moment. As briefs go, that is about as charming as it gets.

"The natural world has always been a rich source of inspiration for Rolls-Royce Bespoke commissions, offering extraordinary forms and colours for our designers and craftspeople to interpret. Our clients continually invite us to explore that inspiration in new and unexpected ways, as this remarkable Private Commission so perfectly demonstrates. The introduction of abalone shell into Rolls-Royce marquetry presented our designers and craftspeople with a fresh creative and technical challenge, resulting in an elegant tribute to the hummingbird’s mesmerising presence."Phil Fabre de la Grange, Head of Bespoke, Rolls-Royce Motor Cars

A FIRST FOR THE MARQUE

Here is the headline for the craft devotees: this is the first time Rolls-Royce has worked abalone shell into its marquetry. Turn the shell over and its nacreous inner surface throws back a blue-green glow that seems to shift as you move. Paired with mother-of-pearl, it becomes plumage. That is a fitting choice, because a hummingbird's colour is not really pigment at all. It comes from microscopic structures in the feathers that split light the way a prism does, so the bird flashes a different shade with every turn of its head. Nature's own special effect has now been translated into shell and wood.

COUNTING FEATHERS

The bird appears twice, once on each Picnic Table, and each one is assembled from 53 pieces of abalone and mother-of-pearl. The wings alone take 18 of them. Between the rear seats, the Rear Waterfall panel carries a botanical motif of 84 pieces, a little garden for the hummingbirds to visit.

THE PATIENCE OF THE HAND

None of it came quickly. The project ran for more than nine months, and abalone made the Bespoke Collective's designers, engineers and craftspeople work for every fragment. It is fine and brittle, and it does not forgive. Six iterations went by before the team settled on a method that worked. In the final approach the pearl and abalone are thinned first, so that each sliver can be seated gently but securely, and then lacquered. That way the shimmer survives the generations of ownership a Phantom is built to see.

Then comes precision that borders on the absurd, in the best possible way. Every piece is laser-cut 0.06 mm larger than its finished size to allow for the burn margin of the laser itself, and then filed to shape by hand. Edges are worked until each one meets its neighbour with no visible seam. On the hummingbird's wings, only a human hand can achieve that fit. After cutting and fitting, each panel needed a further eight hours to complete, and the three panels came to 45 hours in total.

A CABIN IN BLOOM

The woods were chosen to let the shell sing. Ash Burr veneer forms the background of all three marquetry panels. Silver Birch appears within the Picnic Table motifs, and all of it was cut from a single log that was selected and examined closely so its grain would suit the design. The seats are trimmed in Creme Light leather with Moccasin and Tan detailing. Seashell carpets lie underfoot, and a Starlight Headliner glitters overhead, so the hummingbird has a sky to fly in.

DRESSED FOR FLIGHT

Outside, the palette is pure confection: Wildberry on the lower body and White Sands on the upper. A hand-applied White Sands Coachline traces the division between the two, and a hand-painted hummingbird flies along it for the full, generous length of the Phantom Extended. It is a small flourish and a delightful one, and it suits a motor car built around the idea that beauty is often found in the smallest, brightest things.

WHAT MAKES ROLLS-ROYCE CRAFTSMANSHIP UNIQUE

One address, many hands

Every Rolls-Royce is designed and built by hand in one place: the Home of Rolls-Royce at Goodwood, West Sussex. The plant opened on 1 January 2003. At the time, around 300 people built a single car a day. Today more than 2,500 people work there, and the line can produce up to 28 motor cars a day. Rolls-Royce says each car takes more than 600 hours to build, and the most ambitious commissions can take up to four years to develop and complete. The best measure of all this may be longevity: around two-thirds of all Rolls-Royce motor cars ever built are still on the road. Next door, a site extension worth more than £300 million is under way to make more room for Bespoke and Coachbuild. It was declared weathertight in November 2025, and the first technologies are due to move in during 2026.

The Bespoke Collective and the Private Office

The designers, engineers and craftspeople who carry out these commissions work together as the Bespoke Collective. The original Private Office is at Goodwood, where clients sit down with a designer to shape their motor car. Since 2022 the idea has travelled. Dubai opened in July 2022 as the first Private Office outside Goodwood, Shanghai followed in 2023, and New York and Seoul opened in 2024. Each office works by invitation or appointment, with a designer and a client experience manager based in the region. Rolls-Royce says Private Office commissions are typically about 25% higher in value. In 2025, the first full year with all five offices open, commissions through them more than doubled. Phantom Hummingbird is one of them, curated by Private Office Dubai.

A line drawn by hand

The coachline is the fine stripe that runs the length of the body, and it is painted freehand with a brush made from natural materials. Rolls-Royce sets the width at 3 mm on Phantom, Cullinan and Ghost and 4 mm on Spectre, to suit that car's proportions. Since the early years of the Goodwood era, the hand on the brush has belonged to Mark Court, the coachline artisan pictured at work in Rolls-Royce's own PressClub archive. Press profiles have long described the job as his alone. He works with squirrel-hair brushes that he trims to shape himself. Today Rolls-Royce describes a skilled team of artisans applying coachlines and motifs. A motif can be added to the line: a flower, a family crest, a paw print or, on this Phantom, a hummingbird in flight.

A sky in the roof

The Starlight Headliner began as one client's request. He suffered from photophobia, an extreme sensitivity to light, and could only read comfortably under the night sky on his ranch. In 2006 he took delivery of a Phantom with 800 'stars' in its roof. Today a headliner carries between 800 and 1,600 fibre-optic points, each one set by hand at its own depth and angle so the sky seems to twinkle. A standard pattern takes about nine hours. A personal constellation, such as the sky above a particular place on an anniversary, can take more than 17. In 2025 an in-house artist went further for Cullinan Cosmos, creating Rolls-Royce's first fully hand-painted Starlight Headliner in 20 layers of paint over 160 hours.

Wood, leather and the art of marquetry

The department once called the Woodshop is now the Interior Surface Centre. Every veneer in a single car comes from the same tree, so the grain becomes that car's own fingerprint and all of its wood ages together. Veneers rest for at least three days in a humidor held at 23°C and 75% humidity. Royal Walnut is then matched in a chevron at exactly 55 degrees, and Burr Walnut is book-matched like a mirror. Lacquer goes on in layers 0.3 mm thick before being polished to a deep shine. Marquetry and parquetry commissions have ranged from more than 320 hand-placed pieces to a cascade of falling rose petals made from 1,603 pieces of wood. The Hummingbird's abalone is the newest material in that tradition.

In the Interior Trim Centre, every hide is inspected by eye under high-intensity light. Flaws are marked in chalk and cut around, and each leather part is skived from about 1.5 mm to 0.6 mm so its seams sit small and neat. Outside, clients can choose from 44,000 colours, and if none is right, Rolls-Royce will develop a new one and reserve it for that client alone. Each car is then hand-polished for four hours to reach its signature piano finish.

The lady on the bonnet

The Spirit of Ecstasy was sculpted by the artist Charles Robinson Sykes and registered as Rolls-Royce property in 1911. Eleanor Thornton, his muse, is widely held to be her model, though Rolls-Royce happily admits that some mystery remains. In 1920 she won Sykes the gold medal at a Paris contest to find the finest motor car mascot of the day. She is still made by lost-wax casting, a technique more than 5,000 years old, and is offered in solid silver, 24-carat gold plate or black carbon fibre. On every car built at Goodwood she rises gracefully from the grille and retreats out of harm's way at the slightest touch.

Coachbuild: the body itself

Beyond Bespoke lies Coachbuild, where client and marque design the shape of the car together. The modern Coachbuild story runs from Sweptail to Boat Tail and on to Droptail. Boat Tail arrived in 2021 as three commissions sharing one body, each with its own colours and details, and it made its public debut at the Concorso d'Eleganza Villa d'Este. In 2023 came Droptail, the first true two-seater roadster from Rolls-Royce in more than half a century. Only four will be built, among them Arcadia Droptail, which was revealed in 2024.

WHO BUYS A ROLLS-ROYCE

Younger than you might think. When Rolls-Royce refreshed its brand identity in 2020, it said the average age of its clients had fallen to just 43, and it credited that partly to Black Badge, the marque's darker and more assertive alter ego. By 2021, Black Badge cars accounted for 27% of commissions worldwide. Every car built at Goodwood carries some element of Bespoke, so in a real sense every owner is a commissioning client.

The numbers tell the rest of the story. In 2023 Rolls-Royce delivered 6,032 motor cars across more than 50 countries, the most in its history to that point. The total for 2024 was 5,712, its third-best year, and also a record year for Bespoke: the Bespoke content value per car rose 10% on average. In 2025 the marque created 5,664 motor cars. Cullinan was the most requested model and Spectre came second, and the year was the strongest ever in Asia-Pacific. Here is the picture region by region, using only what Rolls-Royce and reputable press have published.

  • The Americas. The United States was the marque's single largest market in 2023, and North America was its largest sales region in 2024 by number of cars handed over. In 2024 North America also ranked close behind the Middle East for average Bespoke value per car. Private Office New York opened in Manhattan's Meatpacking District in 2024. Official showrooms stretch from Toronto and Vancouver to Mexico City, São Paulo and Santiago.
  • Europe. Europe set sales records in both 2023 and 2024, and the UK is its largest single market. In 2024 Spectre was the most requested Rolls-Royce in Europe, Germany achieved record sales, and the Netherlands was among the fast-growing smaller markets. In Portugal, the official network includes Rolls-Royce Motor Cars Porto, and Rolls-Royce publishes its news through a dedicated PressClub Portugal, where the Phantom Hummingbird story also ran.
  • The Middle East. The region is Rolls-Royce's heartland for ambitious Bespoke. In 2023 it was the leading source of the most elaborate and technically demanding commissions, and in 2024 it was the largest Bespoke region by average value per car. The UAE set a national sales record in 2024. Private Office Dubai, the first outside Goodwood, has curated commissions such as Phantom Dentelle and, now, Phantom Hummingbird.
  • Africa. Rolls-Royce reports Africa together with the Middle East, and that combined region achieved record sales in 2024. On the official dealer network, Africa is served by Rolls-Royce Motor Cars Johannesburg at Melrose Arch. The continent also runs through the marque's art programme: Sumayya Vally of the Johannesburg studio Counterspace sat on the Spirit of Ecstasy Challenge jury, and Ghizlane Sahli's winning sculpture was wrapped in gold thread bought from artisans in the Medina of Marrakech.
  • Asia-Pacific. Greater China was the marque's second-largest market in 2023, and Rolls-Royce notes a steady rise in younger clients there. Private Office Shanghai opened that year. Korea powered record Asia-Pacific sales in 2023, and Private Office Seoul opened in Jamsil in 2024. Japan, Malaysia and Thailand were among the markets that grew or set records in 2024. In 2025 Rolls-Royce had its strongest year ever in Asia-Pacific, and India was the largest growth market in APAC South, as reported by ETAuto. Singapore's dealer marked its 20th anniversary with a flagship House of Luxury that it calls the newest and largest in the region. In Australia, official showrooms operate in Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane and Perth.

THE ARTISTS

Behind every Rolls-Royce is a roll call of named hands and minds. Here are the ones the marque itself has put forward.

  • Phil Fabre de la Grange, Head of Bespoke, leads the work that turned a hummingbird into abalone and pearl and is quoted above on the challenge of the new material.
  • The Bespoke Collective is the Goodwood team of designers, engineers and craftspeople who spent more than nine months and six iterations getting the Hummingbird's abalone right. One of its in-house artists painted the Cullinan Cosmos headliner by hand.
  • Mark Court is the coachline artisan pictured in Rolls-Royce's PressClub with his brush. He came to Goodwood from painting pub and village signs.
  • Chris Brownridge, Chief Executive, describes the marque's output in one line: “Every motor car is a singular expression of its owner’s vision.”
  • Charles Robinson Sykes and Eleanor Thornton are the sculptor and the muse behind the Spirit of Ecstasy, the marque's first and most famous work of art.
  • Sondra Perry, an American moving-image artist, won the first Dream Commission from Muse, the Rolls-Royce Art Programme, in 2021.
  • Bi Rongrong, Ghizlane Sahli and Scarlett Yang won the first Muse Spirit of Ecstasy Challenge in 2022, which was dedicated to textiles. Bi made Stitched Urban Skin, layering treated metal, an LED sheet and traditional Chinese crochet. Sahli made Nissa’s Rina (Woman’s Song), a pair of wings built from salvaged plastic wrapped in white silk and gold thread. Yang made Transient Materiality, a one-metre sculpture of iridescent biopolymer ribbons. The works were first shown at Cromwell Place, London, in October 2022. The jury included Anders Warming, then Director of Design at Rolls-Royce, AMBUSH® creative director Yoon Ahn, Design Museum chief executive Tim Marlow and Sumayya Vally of the Johannesburg studio Counterspace.
